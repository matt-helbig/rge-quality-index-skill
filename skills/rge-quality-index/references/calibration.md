# Time-sensitive calibration

Claims that describe the current state of email practice live here so they go stale in one place instead of throughout the rubric. Everything in this file has a shelf life. Review it whenever the rubric is revised, prune what no longer holds, and verify anything consequential against an authoritative source before relying on it. Nothing here is dated on purpose: a date invites treating the entry as fresh until the date passes, when the real question is whether the claim is still true today.

The rest of the rubric is written to outlast this file. If a judgment depends on one of these entries, say so in the review.

## Regulatory context

A US Department of Justice rule makes WCAG 2.1 AA a legal requirement for public entities. Scope and compliance deadlines vary by entity size, and the landscape moves. For government, higher-education, and public-institution senders, accessibility failures carry regulatory exposure on top of their scoring impact — note that exposure in P5 rather than inventing an extra deduction for it. Confirm current scope before telling a sender they have a legal problem; QI does not certify compliance.

## Shifting expectations

- **Dark mode is a baseline expectation, not an advanced technique.** Score observed negligence as a craft failure rather than a missed bonus.
- **Interactive email keeps getting easier.** What read as exceptional a few years ago is approaching ordinary. Calibrate interactivity credit downward over time, and credit the contribution rather than the technique.
- **All-image construction is trending toward disqualification.** The mechanical rule — P2 ceiling of 2.9, which forces final ≤3.4 — reflects current strictness. Expect it to tighten.
- **Generative tooling is common in email production.** This does not change the rubric: judge the output, never its origin, and never infer authorship from style. What it does change is scarcity — as volume rises, evidence that the sender actually understands the reader is the rarer signal. Weight it accordingly.

## Visual trend inventory

These treatments are currently common enough that their presence alone is not a distinctiveness signal:

> rounded corners · wavy dividers · organic blob shapes · heavy gradients · oversized display type

The rule that governs them lives in P1 and does not change: a widely-adopted treatment used as an accent can be positive, and the same treatment used as the whole design strategy is neutral-to-negative. What changes is *which* treatments are ubiquitous. Revisit this list, not the rule.

Do not read the list as a deduction trigger. An email using a treatment well is not penalized for the treatment being popular, and a reviewer's fatigue with a look is not evidence that a reader shares it.
