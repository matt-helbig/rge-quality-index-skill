# Industry context

Read when industry or audience context changes the judgment. Context adjusts how evidence is interpreted, never the numerical weights. Do not infer a brand's audience, sophistication, or performance from its category alone, and do not apply an entry here to an email that only superficially resembles the category.

**SaaS / B2B.** Product UI, in-context dashboards, and concrete workflows make a benefit understandable, and "show the product, not just the promise" is the category norm — credit it in Strategy when the visual does that work. Abstract imagery is not inferior when it serves the idea. Authority content (original research, benchmarks, surveys) is a legitimate centerpiece rather than an add-on. Expert audiences are hostile to hype: match vocabulary to actual expertise, score unearned superlatives in P3, and do not require a casual tone.

**E-commerce / retail.** High SKU density is not a deduction — apply the Retail Density Safeguard and judge hierarchy control, offer clarity, and prioritization logic instead of raw product count. Product grids may be the intended browsing experience rather than competing CTAs. Product photography as hero is expected in the category. Browse and cart-abandonment trigger logic is where real strategic lift shows up; recommendations earn credit only when relevance is supported.

**Fashion / luxury.** Restraint, conventional grids, and expressive imagery are frequently deliberate. Design execution and brand cohesion carry more of the judgment here, so apply Contextual Brand Evaluation and assess distinctiveness against the brand's own parameters rather than against louder categories. Live text is genuinely rare in this category — call it out as a positive when present. Category convention never waives accessibility.

**Beauty / personal care.** Before-and-after imagery, ingredient explanations, and consultation or quiz-result flows are high-value formats when the claims are understandable and supported. A data-shaped visual is not evidence by itself.

**Public sector, education, and public institutions.** These senders carry regulatory exposure on accessibility that most senders do not, so a failure there has a consequence beyond its score. Note the exposure in P5 rather than inventing an extra deduction for it, and confirm current scope before telling a sender they have a legal problem. QI does not certify compliance.

**Health / wellness / medical.** Favor clarity, sensitivity, and substantiated claims; clinical language should be earned rather than gestured at. Technical vocabulary may be correct for specialist audiences. Accessibility matters more than average here, because the audience is more likely to include disabled readers. Do not certify clinical or legal claims through this rubric.

**Food / beverage.** Sensory imagery, recipes, and ingredient storytelling deliver real value in this category and are worth scoring generously on editorial merit. Animation is optional, and earns credit for its contribution and its fallback rather than for existing.

**Non-profit / charity.** Emotional authenticity outweighs production polish, and founder or leadership voice is appropriate. Assess clarity of the ask, credible impact, and respectful storytelling. Donation paths should be low-friction and honest about where money goes. Commercial revenue is not the necessary outcome — a donation, a volunteer action, or useful information may be the purpose.

**Editorial / B2B newsletters.** Voice distinctiveness and editorial point of view matter more here than in promotional email. Evaluate selection, perspective, substance, and readable organization. Many links and a complete in-email reading experience are legitimate outcomes, not dilution.

**Multilingual audiences.** More than one language in a send can be intentional and inclusive rather than a segmentation failure. Evaluate reading order, language identification, and usable navigation between versions. Do not assume splitting into separate sends is always preferable.
