# Program Maturity Signals

Diagnostics for a **lifecycle program**, not a single email.

Everything here requires context that one email rarely exposes: send cadence,
sequence position, revenue mix, preference architecture. Load this file only
when you genuinely have that context — a program audit, a multi-email teardown,
a client engagement with reporting access.

**Scoring rule:** these signals adjust Pillar 5 *only when observable*. When
they aren't, apply P5's incomplete-observability default of 3.0 and note the
assumption. Never infer program maturity from a single email, and never
fabricate strategic intent to avoid the default. An email is not penalized for
belonging to a program you cannot see.

---

## Campaign-to-flow revenue balance

Best-practice programs run roughly **50% campaign revenue, 50% flow revenue**.
Heavy campaign skew signals lifecycle immaturity: the brand is manually pushing
sends rather than building automation that earns while unattended.

Requires revenue reporting. Not inferable from an email.

## Onboarding sequence length

Best practice is **3–5 emails**. Nine or more indicates over-engineering and
likely fatigue.

Score the individual email on its own merits regardless. Sequence length is a
note on the program, never a deduction against one email in it.

## Preference management

Programs offering **topic, frequency, or format choice** instead of binary
subscribe/unsubscribe demonstrate lifecycle sophistication — they treat the
relationship as adjustable rather than terminal.

This one is partly observable: if a preference center is linked from the
footer, credit it in P5 from the email alone.

## Loyalty without noise

Fewer emails at higher relevance runs counter to volume-based lifecycle
marketing. Credit programs that clearly prioritize engagement quality over send
frequency when that context is identifiable.

Requires cadence data.

## Seasonal program patterns

Brands anchored to **ownable micro-moments** — a founding date, a product
anniversary, a category-specific occasion — score better on distinctiveness and
Strategy than brands running the standard promotional calendar.

At the program level this is a calendar question: does the brand own its
moments, or rent everyone else's? At the email level, the question is narrower
and stays in P5 proper — does this seasonal hook connect to the brand's actual
purpose, or is it a theme applied to unrelated content?
